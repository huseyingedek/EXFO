Two traders with exactly the same strategy can achieve completely different results. The difference is often not in the analysis but in the behaviour: do they stick to their rules, even when it gets tense?

## The most common pitfalls

- **Loss aversion** — a loss feels heavier than a gain of the same size. As a result, traders let losing positions run too long and close winners too early.
- **FOMO** (*fear of missing out*) — jumping in because price is already moving fast, without your setup being present.
- **Revenge trading** — opening a new trade straight after a loss to ‘win it back’, often with a bigger position.
- **Overconfidence** — raising your risk or dropping your rules after a few winning trades.
- **Confirmation bias** — only looking for information that confirms what you already believe.

## A plan that protects you from yourself

You can't switch off emotions, but you can keep them out of your decisions. You do that with fixed rules that you set in advance, when you are calm:

1. **A trading plan** — which markets, which setups, which hours and how much risk per trade.
2. **A checklist for every trade** — every box ticked? Only then do you enter.
3. **A daily loss limit** — for example, stop after two losing trades or after −2% in a day.
4. **A fixed routine** — the same preparation, at the same time, every day.

## The trading journal

A journal is your most important learning tool. For every trade, record:

- date, market, direction and position size;
- the reason for entry, plus your stop-loss and target;
- the result in euros and in R (multiples of your risk);
- how you felt before, during and after the trade;
- what you would do differently next time.

After twenty or thirty trades you'll see patterns you would otherwise never notice — for example that you mostly lose on Friday afternoons, or right after a loss.

## Process over outcome

A good trade is one in which you followed your plan — whether it made a profit or a loss. In the short term, chance plays a big role; in the long term, your discipline makes the difference. So judge yourself on the quality of your decisions, not on the outcome of a single trade.
